This article isn't about ethics. There's a separate and active debate on [AI harms](https://stevefenton.co.uk/blog/2026/04/generative-ai-harms/) that the industry must face up to, or that governments will step in to regulate. Each of us has a voice in that large-scale debate, but we also need something on a smaller scale.

We need some etiquette.

Though creators often share a [personal statement about their AI use](https://stevefenton.co.uk/blog/2023/02/facing-reality-ai/), those around them have often made different choices. As more people experiment with voice and chat interactions with Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini, and share more output from these tools, they often unwittingly trip over a mismatch in social expectations. This mismatch comes from a lack of etiquette.

Etiquette is less about the big companies versus the people and more about how we interact with each other as humans in society. There is always delightful variation in even the most trivial rules of etiquette, but on the whole, people tend to get the important stuff right. Most of us know that holding a door open for someone is polite etiquette.

If you've done posh dining, you may have encountered silver service, where servers dish up from your left, and a whole set of cutlery of different shapes and sizes borders your plate. When you dine this way, there are many more layers of etiquette regarding utensil choice, the use of spoons with soup, and where to place napkins.

In these early days of generative AI, we seem to be at the "ripping flesh from a carcass" stage of our etiquette development. With AI being such a divisive topic, the need for terms of engagement is urgent.

## General guidance

Our first layer of etiquette involves forming a model around AI tools that moves it from "exceptional magic" into a category we already recognize. Depending on the task you're undertaking, this likely differs.

For example, if you produce an internal strategy document, you should model your behaviour on the accepted norms of creating that document before AI. People reading the document would expect the contents to be accurate, the strategy to contain strong opinions grounded in your knowledge and experience, and for it to be in a shape that's ready to share. It was normal to review the document before you shared it to check that it was the best reflection of your thought process.

Sometimes, just thinking about the task will be enough to ground whatever you do with AI in a set of well-understood expectations.

### The "you made it" principle

When you've produced a document or implemented a feature using AI, you made it. This was true when you used a word processor (even when it had spelling and grammar tools), and it was true of your code editor (even when it had auto-complete). AI is another tool, so the responsibility of what you produce and how it affects other people remains yours.

That means you have to consider the following:

- How does the output reflect on you professionally?  
- Did it save you time without introducing quality problems?  
- Have you transferred a burden of review onto someone else?

A real example may help. A sixteen-page strategy document was sent to me, and I spent a solid half hour reading it end to end. Then I went through and added annotations with questions and comments. That's the best part of an hour, and that gets multiplied for each recipient who diligently reviews its contents. Quality problems caused by a lack of producer diligence damage your reputation and create a net time-saving loss for your organization.

Imagine how you'd feel after spending real time on something like this, and then having its author respond to your comments and annotations with, "Oh, I used Claude to generate that, and I haven't read it yet\!" You should always be the first person to review what you created.

### The "I made it" principle

Here's a corollary to the "you made it" principle based on giving people feedback on their output. We all have access to these AI tools, should we want to use them, so suggesting we "run it through AI" or passing it through Claude on our behalf to "polish it" is disrespectful. You might use AI as part of your review, but the bar for feedback requires more effort than a simple copy/paste operation that the original producer could have done.

Once again, the three guiding questions can help you shape your feedback:

- How does the feedback you give reflect on you professionally?  
- Did it save you time without introducing quality problems?  
- Have you transferred a burden of review onto someone else?

When I was discussing the idea of AI etiquette with others in the software community, I came across several stories of people writing content and having an AI-generated version sent back to them. In every case, the problem was the same. The process of filtering human-authored work through AI meant it lost the one thing that made it worth reading: its humanity. Many writers have realized that reader trust is valuable and scarce, so authentic writing is now more important than ever.

The more abstract idea sitting behind these principles is that low-effort production and low-effort review aren't helpful or welcome. If you don't have anything smarter to add than the AI tools, it may be better to sit back and let others work on it. This is why many AI-generated web articles are so worthless: you already got that exact text from the search engine's AI overview, and there is no additional expertise in the article to make it worth the reading time.

## Rules of etiquette

Until we have broadly accepted terms of engagement around AI, teams and organizations will need to develop their own guidelines. If they do this openly, as [Oxide](https://rfd.shared.oxide.computer/rfd/0576) has done, it will help humans develop the much-needed shared etiquette. At Octopus, we have a [set of values](https://handbook.octopus.com/getting-oriented/values) that helps us navigate these decisions, not least "doing a smaller number of things at high quality," which has been instructive over the past few years..

Perhaps the most abstract view on this topic is to question which authentic part of yourself you have brought to the work. If you're not adding value over and above what can be statistically produced, you're doing a disservice to your colleagues. It also makes you the extinction target for this kind of technology, because if anyone could prompt their way to a similar result, what you do isn't needed.

If you create something with AI, you should be the first to read and review it. What you send to others reflects on you and your work. When you receive feedback, saying "Claude wrote it" isn't a defence. Don't alter other people's work with AI without their permission, especially when the producer is bringing their authenticity to the work. And often, not doing something at all can be better than rushing it through with AI.
